The Reality Behind Brazil's Favelas

Years of uneven city planning have left many favela communities without reliable infrastructure, clean sanitation, or steady public safety. Even with these issues, residents still organize and support each other every day.

Formal City Areas

Paved streets, city sewer lines, working streetlights, and faster emergency access.

Informal Hillside Areas

Steep narrow walkways, spotty utility hookups, and less public sanitation coverage.

Uneven city spending often separates formal neighborhoods from hillside or outer favelas, which affects daily health, travel, and job opportunities.

Infrastructure Gaps & Sanitation Issues

Basic services like clean water, sewage pipes, trash pickup, and reliable transit are essential for everyday health (SDG 3) and financial stability (SDG 1 & SDG 10).

Sanitation & Health

Sewage & Water Access

Brazil's 2022 Census showed 62.5% of people lived in homes connected to a sewage network, with big gaps across different regions, income levels, and race. In crowded informal areas, open drainage and weak water pressure raise the risk of waterborne sickness.

Source: IBGE 2022 Census & Atlas of Basic Sanitation.

City Access

Hillsides, Streets & Utilities

Many favelas were built on steep hills or flood-prone areas where heavy trucks and standard utility lines cannot easily reach. Narrow alleys and steep stairs can slow down ambulances, garbage pickup, and daily commutes to work.

Impact: Longer commutes and higher delivery costs for families and local small businesses.

Inequality & Opportunity

Service Gaps & Poverty

Missing infrastructure does not happen on its own. It usually comes alongside fewer nearby clinics, underfunded schools, fewer formal jobs, and unclear land titles, making it harder for families to get ahead.

SDG Link: Connected directly to UN SDGs 1, 3, and 10.

Crime Data & Pacification History

Violence in and around favelas comes from years of territory fights between armed groups and militias, along with strict policing policies that had mixed results.

Territory Fights & Crime Patterns

Crime rates are not the same in every favela or neighborhood. Public health and crime studies in Rio de Janeiro show that violence spikes most around disputed borders where rival trafficking groups and militias fight for control, putting regular residents and passersby in danger.

  • Rival group turf fights cause sharp spikes in local shootings and violence.
  • Residents in low-income communities often take the biggest hit from these conflicts.
  • Data Note: Always compare per-100,000-resident rates and exact reporting years when checking state public security statistics (ISP).

Pacification (UPP) & Mixed Results

Starting in 2008 with Santa Marta and expanding before major international events, Rio de Janeiro set up Pacifying Police Units (UPPs) to take back territory from armed groups, place permanent police stations, and pair security with social programs.

  • Evaluations found fewer police-related deaths in many UPP areas during early years, though changes in overall homicide rates varied across different communities.
  • Budget cuts, financial crises, and resident complaints about heavy-handed policing and missing social services made it hard to keep the program going long term.
